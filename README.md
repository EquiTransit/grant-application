## Summary

Transit systems are a key component of urban public policy. These
systems intersect with broader transportation and urban land use issues
and are therefore of great interest and practical import. In general
terms, transit systems generate accessibility to opportunities that
contribute to the economic operation of urban regions. But this is not
the only service that transit systems provide, since they use space more
efficiently than low-capacity vehicles (mainly private automobiles), and
thus help to reduce congestion and result in lower emissions per capita.
Furthermore, transit is an obligated form of transport for many members
of the public who may not be able to own or use a private vehicle and
increasingly supports many urban residents who choose not to own a
vehicle. As such, transit systems also help to reduce
transportation-related social exclusion, and support democratic
participation in many essential activities.

Despite their importance in public policy, transit services are often
offered without explicit mention of the principles that underpin their
provision. Implicitly, transit is often seen as an economic service that
must remain fiscally responsible. But while fiscal responsibility is an
important consideration, it is not by any means the only one. In
reality, many of the benefits of transit systems are non-monetary, and a
tendency to see them mainly as economic services leads to a narrow
approach that can blunt their attractiveness. Particularly, there are
numerous examples where this leads to major inequities in terms of the
distribution of the benefits and the costs of public transportation,
while ignoring its role as a facilitator of democratic participation in
society.

Planning for transit systems that are equitable and promote ideals of
transportation equity and justice is an urgent task, especially as the
limits of highly individualized transportation systems become clear.
EquiTransit proposes to re-envision transit from a broader, more just
perspective, and aims to create the knowledge and tools needed to
realize this vision. This project follows on the heels of recent
research initiatives in Canada, particularly the SSHRC-funded project
Mobilizing Justice, which have broken new ground and made considerable
progress towards more inclusive, equitable, and just transportation
systems in Canada. EquiTransit builds on these foundations with a more
targeted focus on public transportation.

Accordingly, the objective of EquiTransit is to generate novel,
theoretically-sound and analytics-based equity indicators useful to
assess the performance of public transit systems in Canada.
Theoretically, we will use a framework of justice that emphasizes the
5WH approach (why, what, who, where, when, how). To ensure that our
indicators are pertinent to the question of “why equity?”, we propose a
Codesign approach, whereby the research team will work with transit
operators and users in the explicit identification of the rationale and
motivation for the development and use of indicators. We plan to use
cutting-edge geospatial technologies to investigate the questions “who
seeks equity, and where and when?”. Further, we propose to create
open-source tools, including equity-informed planning and assessment
tools, and place them at the fingertips of stakeholders at the lowest
possible cost. Finally, in the spirit of Codesign, the effectiveness of
the tools will be assessed in consultation with stakeholders, so that
refinements can be incorporated in successive releases of the tools. In
this way, EquiTransit aims to produce actionable knowledge to support
the development of more equitable transit in Canada, to the benefit of
urban residents, democratic governance, and democratic participation in
Canadian society.
